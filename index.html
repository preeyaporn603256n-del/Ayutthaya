Ayutthaya

The Golden Capital of Siam

For 417 years, a city of rivers, rice and trade ruled Siam, until Burmese armies burned it in 1767.

Why here? Three rivers

The island sits where the Chao Phraya, Pasak and Lopburi meet.

  • Defence: rivers ringed the city, and it sat above the tidal bore, out of reach of sea-going warships.
  • Food: floods fertilised the rice fields, feeding a large population.
  • Trade: river access to the sea, midway between India and China.
  • Flood control: straight north–south canals drained the rainy-season water.

Timeline

  1. 1350
    U-Thong moves his court from Uthong to escape an epidemic and founds Ayutthaya, named after Ayodhya, home of Rama in the Ramayana. He is declared king before dying in 1369.
  2. 14th–18th c.
    The city grows into one of the world's largest and most cosmopolitan, and control spreads to present-day Cambodia and Laos.
  3. c. 1500s
    Portugal is the first Western power to arrive, in the reign of Ramathibodi II. Spain, Holland, England and France follow.
  4. 1686
    A French embassy of Louis XIV reaches King Narai's court. Its mapmaker draws the city as "Judia".
  5. 1767
    Burmese forces destroy the city. Survivors flee south and set up a new capital at Thonburi. Ayutthaya lies empty.
  6. 1782
    Rama I founds Rattanakosin (Bangkok), copying Ayutthaya's plan. Builders came from Ayutthaya, and bricks were taken from its walls and temples. Bangkok's formal name still contains "Ayutthaya".
  7. 1908
    King Chulalongkorn keeps the island as public land and begins surveys and excavations.
  8. 1935–1976
    The Fine Arts Department registers 69 sites (1935) and gazettes 2.9 km² as a historical site (1976).
  9. 1991
    UNESCO lists the Historic City of Ayutthaya on 13 December.
  10. 2011 & 2017
    Floods damage Wat Phra Si Sanphet. In 2017 CyArk scans it with LiDAR and photogrammetry to track the subsidence.

The 1686 French map

Drawn by R.P. Placide, geographer to Louis XIV's embassy to King Narai.

  • It traces the embassy's ship through the Straits of Sunda, between Sumatra and Java, into the Gulf of Siam and up the Chao Phraya.
  • The French called the city "Judia". The map shows it beside the river, sheltered by forests and mountains.
  • Maps like this one, drawn by foreign envoys, are how UNESCO knows the city's original street and canal pattern.

Roads ran beside canals, and canals split the island into zones for temples, palaces and homes. UNESCO calls the water-management system technologically advanced and unique in the world. The wall began as earth under U-Thong and became brick under King Maha Chakkraphat.

How the kingdom worked

Absolute monarchy

The king ruled all. King Borommatrailokkanat split government into military and civil, with four civil ministries (chatusadom).

CityPalaceTreasury & tradeFarming

Society

Nobles above, then peasants, then slaves. Peasants belonged to a master and served the crown about 6 months a year, or paid a fee called suay. Slaves served for life.

Economy

Rice fed the people and was exported. The royal warehouse held a monopoly on key goods. Exports also included celadon, ivory, leather, timber and spices.

A crossroads of East and West

The court swapped ambassadors with Versailles, Delhi, Japan and China.

Wat Phra Si Sanphet

Ayutthaya's most important temple served the royal family and held royal ceremonies only, with no monks' quarters. Its three bell-shaped chedis are believed to hold the ashes of three kings (Trailok, Borom Ratchathirat III and Ramathibodi II). The gold-covered 16 m Buddha was carried off by the Burmese. The three chedis are a symbol of Thailand today.

Other landmarks: tall prang towers, Wat Mahathat, and the mural paintings in the crypt of Wat Ratchaburana.

Ayutthaya today

  • A historical park under Thailand's Ancient Monuments Act (1961, amended 1992), run by the Fine Arts Department.
  • Temples now sit between homes and shops, so an extension is being prepared to cover the whole 18th-century island footprint.
  • From 1994 to 2001, 862.73 million baht went into projects under the master plan, from government and private funds.
